Why murmurs happen

  • Blood flow becomes turbulent when it is forced through a narrow or leaking opening by a pressure difference.
  • The murmur lasts as long as that gradient drives flow, so the timing comes from the pressure traces.
  • Systole runs from S1 (mitral and tricuspid closure) to S2 (aortic then pulmonary closure).

Systolic murmurs

  • Ejection systolic (crescendo-decrescendo): aortic stenosis, HOCM, innocent flow murmur.
  • Pansystolic (S1 to S2): mitral regurgitation, tricuspid regurgitation, VSD.
  • AS radiates to the carotids; MR to the axilla.

Diastolic murmurs

  • Aortic regurgitation: early diastolic, decrescendo, high pitched; left sternal edge, sitting forward, breath held out.
  • Mitral stenosis: loud S1, opening snap, low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble at the apex; left lateral position, bell.
  • Diastolic murmurs are almost always pathological and need investigating.

Bedside manoeuvres

  • Breath in: right-sided murmurs louder (Carvallo's sign in TR). Left-sided murmurs are louder in expiration.
  • Valsalva or standing: most murmurs softer; HOCM louder.
  • Squatting: most murmurs louder; HOCM softer. Handgrip: MR, VSD and AR louder; AS and HOCM softer.
