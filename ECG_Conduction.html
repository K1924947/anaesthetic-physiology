Why the ECG looks like that

The heart is the cause, the leads are the effect. Teaching tool, not clinical guidance.

0 ms after the SA node fires

QRS 90 ms

For anaesthetist review
  • One activation-time map drives everything: the arrows, dots and chevrons on the heart, the net vector, every lead trace, the bump labels and the step-through story. Every trace is the projection of that one summed vector (a single dipole) on the lead's axis, so polarity always matches the arrow. Not recordings; real ECGs also contain non-dipole and electrode-proximity effects.
  • The map is a flat 2D slice: muscle points on a 7-unit grid (about 2.3 mm), scale chosen by hand so the heart is about 10 cm long. Speeds from Klabunde: bundle branches 2 m/s, Purkinje fibres 4 m/s, ventricular muscle 0.5 m/s (one speed for all muscle, no fibre direction).
  • Because a flat slice makes long cell-to-cell routes longer than the real 3D routes, muscle spread beyond one wall thickness is treated as 1.5 times quicker (a model choice, not a measured speed).
  • Hand-set model choices: where each fast path starts exciting muscle (insulated His bundle and upper bundle branches; right bundle reaching the septum low down; LV Purkinje network branching half way down the posterior fascicle); the coupling distance (12 units); how far muscle spread runs before it is called "slow, cell to cell" (RV 22 units, elsewhere 42); the out-of-plane (front to back) direction given to each region; the RV counted at 0.75 because its wall is thinner than drawn; and slow fronts counted 3 times, because in a slice a slow front crossing a wall shows as a short line but in the heart it is a broad sheet.
  • One calibration constant for all conditions: normal lead II R wave set to 1.2 mV. Chest leads share one gain.
  • The atria are not mapped: the P wave is two hand-set components (right then left atrium).
  • Repolarisation is a simple rule from the same map: fast-route muscle gives a concordant T (epicardium recovers first); slow-route muscle gives a secondary ST/T opposite to its own depolarisation. Typical patterns; individual patients vary.
  • Bump labels: each QRS deflection of the focus lead is split at clear notches and credited to the muscle regions whose own projection on that lead has the bump's sign; the top one or two are named. A teaching attribution, not a measured one.
  • Timings in this model: PR about 155 ms (165 ms to the first muscle in LBBB, where the first muscle is reached via the right bundle); QRS 75 ms normal, 160 ms RBBB, 195 ms LBBB from first to last muscle activation (the low-amplitude tail makes the readout longer than a measured surface QRS).
  • Known gaps: in RBBB the model's R wave in I and aVL is smaller than typical (aVL is mostly negative), and LBBB inferior leads are more multiphasic than usual.
  • The frontal lead lines on the heart and the chest wheel show the same vector: frontal (x, y) on the heart, horizontal (x, z) on the wheel; dashed during ST and T. Chest lead angles are standard approximations.
  • The heart is a flat, simplified four-chamber cut-through; valve, vessel and conduction positions are schematic.
  • Time is stretched so one beat fills each panel: thin vertical lines are 40 ms, horizontal lines 0.5 mV. The ms readout is the model's true time.
