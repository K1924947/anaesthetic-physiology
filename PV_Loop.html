Revise this topic

Normal LV (Guyton, Ch. 9)
  • EDV about 110 to 120 mL · ESV about 40 to 50 mL · SV about 70 mL · EF usually about 60%
  • AV opens at a little over 80 mmHg · arterial systolic about 120 mmHg
  • Loop width = SV · loop area = external stroke work
Three loop determinants
  • Preload: rightward, usually wider (Frank-Starling: stretch improves actin-myosin overlap)
  • Afterload: taller and narrower; ESV rises along the same ESPVR; SV falls
  • Contractility: ESPVR steepens or flattens (Ees); sympathetic stimulation can as much as double force
Energy and coupling
  • PVA (loop area + potential energy) relates to oxygen use per beat
  • Tension rises with pressure × chamber diameter, so a dilated ventricle is energetically costly
  • Ea = Pes / SV · coupling is Ea/Ees (often about 0.6 in humans; isolated-heart optimum often quoted as 1)
Valve and failure patterns
  • AS: tall, narrow, high LV pressure
  • MR: no vertical iso-contraction; large total SV
  • AR: no vertical iso-relaxation; low AV-opening pressure
  • MS: normal shape, small EDV and SV (filling limited)
  • Systolic failure: flat ESPVR, dilated, low EF
  • Diastolic dysfunction: steep EDPVR, high EDP, preserved EF
1. What does the width of the LV pressure-volume loop represent, and what does the enclosed area represent?
Width is stroke volume (EDV − ESV). The enclosed area is external stroke work (volume-pressure work). Guyton and Hall, 12th ed., Ch. 9; Power and Kam, 4th ed., Ch. 26.
2. How is contractility represented on the pressure-volume diagram, and is that relationship load-independent?
Contractility is represented by the slope of ESPVR (Ees). Increased inotropy steepens ESPVR and usually shifts it up and left; reduced inotropy flattens it. Ees is a relatively load-independent measure of chamber contractility, not an absolute one, and ESPVR can lose linearity at extremes. Bastos et al., 2020.
3. Define Ea and Ees, and state how they are coupled in the normal heart.
Ees is the slope of ESPVR (mmHg/mL). Ea is Pes / SV, a lumped afterload term. Their ratio, Ea/Ees, indexes ventricular-arterial coupling. Isolated-heart studies often find maximal stroke work near Ea = Ees. In humans, Ea/Ees commonly sits around 0.6 when work and efficiency are near optimal; values clearly above 1 imply mismatch. Bastos et al., 2020.
4. Why are the isovolumetric phases drawn as vertical lines, and when would that be wrong?
They are vertical because both the mitral and aortic valves are closed, so LV volume is fixed while pressure rises or falls (Guyton’s periods of isovolumic contraction and relaxation). The left-hand limb is not vertical in mitral regurgitation. The right-hand limb is not vertical in aortic regurgitation.
5. How does an isolated increase in preload change the loop, and what is the mechanism?
The loop shifts right and usually widens: EDV rises, and SV rises by the Frank-Starling mechanism if the ventricle is within physiological limits. Guyton’s explanation is that stretch brings actin and myosin to a more nearly optimal overlap, so force rises. End-systolic points still lie on the same ESPVR if contractility is unchanged.
6. How is the pressure-volume area related to myocardial oxygen consumption?
Guyton states that oxygen consumption is directly related to the total shaded area in the volume-pressure diagram: external work plus a potential-energy term (the extra work that could be done if the ventricle emptied completely). Later work calls this the pressure-volume area (PVA) and finds a linear PVA–MVO2 relation at a given contractile state (Suga; Bastos et al., 2020). This is larger than the loop area alone.
