Read an ECG step by step

Once you can name the parts of a beat, read a tracing in this order: rate, then rhythm, then axis. The PR, QRS, QT and QTc intervals are covered on the labelled beat and in the normal values table at the bottom of the page.

1. Rate

On standard paper (25 mm/s), a 10-second lead II rhythm strip is 50 large squares long. For a regular rhythm, rate = 300 / the number of large squares between two R waves. For an irregular rhythm, count the QRS complexes in the 10-second strip and multiply by 6.

Strip 1 · 10 s strip

Scroll the strip sideways if you cannot see all 10 seconds.

Usual normal

Adult rate 60 to 100 /min.

Why it matters clinically

  • A rate under 60 /min is bradycardia; over 100 /min is tachycardia.
  • An irregularly irregular rate, with no P waves, occurs in atrial fibrillation.

Sources: LITFL, ECG rate interpretation · LITFL, rhythm evaluation.

2. Rhythm

Ask two questions. Is the rhythm regular or irregular? And is there a P wave before every QRS? March the calipers from R to R: if they keep landing on each R wave, the rhythm is regular. A regular rhythm with a P wave before every QRS is consistent with sinus rhythm. An irregularly irregular rhythm with no P waves is atrial fibrillation.

Strip 1

Scroll the strip sideways if you cannot see all 10 seconds.

Usual normal

Sinus rhythm is regular, at 60 to 100 /min in adults, with a P wave before every QRS and a constant PR interval. P waves are upright in I and II, and inverted in aVR.

Why it matters clinically

  • A P wave before every QRS, with a stable PR interval, is consistent with sinus rhythm.
  • Absent P waves and an irregularly irregular QRS rhythm occur in atrial fibrillation.

Sources: LITFL, rhythm evaluation · LITFL, normal sinus rhythm.

3. Axis

Cardiac axis is the net direction of ventricular depolarisation in the frontal plane. Usual normal is -30° to +90°. Drag the arrow on the hexaxial wheel, or use the buttons and arrow keys, and watch leads I, II and aVF change. The quadrant method uses I and aVF: if both are positive, the axis is between 0° and +90°. Lead II then separates a still-normal axis (-30° to 0°) from left axis deviation (less than -30°).     Usual normal

    QRS axis between -30° and +90°. Left axis deviation is less than -30°. Right axis deviation is greater than +90°. An extreme (northwest) axis is when leads I and aVF are both negative.

    Why it matters clinically

    • Left axis deviation can be seen in left ventricular hypertrophy, left bundle branch block, inferior infarction, and left anterior fascicular block.
    • Right axis deviation can be seen in right ventricular hypertrophy, acute right ventricular strain (for example pulmonary embolism), and chronic lung disease.

    Sources: LITFL, ECG axis interpretation.

    Normal values at a glance
    Normal values for the main parts of an ECG beat, with the source for each figure
    Part Represents Usual normal Source
    P wave Atrial depolarisation Duration under 0.12 s; height under 2.5 mm in the limb leads. LITFL, P wave; Klabunde
    PR interval Start of P to start of QRS (atrial depolarisation plus AV delay) 0.12 to 0.20 s (3 to 5 small squares). LITFL, PR interval; Klabunde
    QRS complex Ventricular depolarisation Under 0.12 s (3 small squares). LITFL, QRS interval; Klabunde
    ST segment Ventricles fully depolarised; roughly the action potential plateau Isoelectric at the J point, judged against the T-P segment. LITFL, ST segment; Klabunde
    T wave Ventricular repolarisation Upright in all leads except aVR and V1. Height under 5 mm in the limb leads and under 10 mm in the chest leads. LITFL, T wave; Klabunde
    QT and QTc Ventricular depolarisation and repolarisation, corrected for rate QTc under 440 ms in men and under 460 ms in women. Over 500 ms carries a high risk of torsades de pointes. LITFL, QT interval; Klabunde
    RR and rate Time from one R wave to the next; used to calculate rate 60 to 100 /min. At 25 mm/s: rate = 300 / large squares between R waves. LITFL, rate interpretation

    The PR segment (the flat line between the P wave and the QRS) is covered on the labelled beat. The small U wave after the T wave is included there too, but it is not needed to read a basic ECG.

    QTc, briefly

    The uncorrected QT shortens as the heart rate rises, so we estimate what the QT would be at 60 /min. The most commonly used formula is Bazett: QTc = QT ÷ √RR, with RR in seconds.

    Bazett over-corrects above 100 /min and under-corrects below 60 /min. Outside that range, Fridericia or Framingham is often used instead. A useful rule of thumb is that a normal QT is under half the preceding RR interval. Do not use a single QTc value alone to make clinical decisions.
