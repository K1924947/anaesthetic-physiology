    Revise this topic

    Fast response: ventricle, atria, Purkinje

    • Phase 4: rest about −90 mV, near EK; IK1 open
    • Phase 0: INa (Nav1.5) from threshold about −70 mV rapidly to about +20 mV
    • Phase 1: Ito notch · Phase 2: ICa-L balances IK (plateau) · Phase 3: IKr, IKs, IK1
    • AP lasts 200 to 400 ms; long refractory period

    Slow response: SA and AV node

    • No stable rest: maximum diastolic about −60 mV
    • Phase 4: If (HCN), then T-type (about −50 mV) and L-type (about −40 mV) Ca2+; IK falls
    • Phase 0: ICa-L from −40 to −30 mV, much slower upstroke · Phase 3: IK
    • Phase 4 slope sets the rate: intrinsic 100 to 110 /min, about 60 to 80 /min with resting vagal tone

    From cell to ECG

    • Ventricular phase 0 → QRS; phase 2 → ST segment; phase 3 → T wave
    • Atrial phase 0 → P wave; atrial repolarisation hidden in the QRS
    • SA phase 4 slope → rate (RR interval); faster upstroke → faster conduction
    • Longer ventricular AP → longer QT

    What changes it

    • High K+: rest less negative, slow phase 0, fast phase 3: peaked T, wide QRS, slow rate
    • Low K+: slow phase 3: flat T, U waves, long QU · Ca2+: high shortens, low lengthens the plateau and QT
    • β1: steeper nodal phase 4 · ACh (M2): hyperpolarised, flatter
    • Drugs: I phase 0 (QRS) · III phase 3 (QT) · II, IV, adenosine on the nodal phase 4 and AV node (rate, PR)
    1. Why does a ventricular myocyte rest near −90 mV?
    At rest the membrane is mostly permeable to K+ (IK1 open; fast Na+ and L-type Ca2+ channels closed), so it sits close to the K+ equilibrium potential, about −96 mV at an extracellular K+ of 4 mmol/L. The Na+/K+-ATPase keeps the gradients. Klabunde, Membrane potentials and Non-pacemaker action potentials.
    2. Compare fast-response and slow-response action potentials.
    Fast response (atrial and ventricular myocytes, Purkinje fibres): stable rest near −90 mV, a rapid Na+-driven phase 0 and a plateau. Slow response (SA and AV nodes): no stable rest, a pacemaker phase 4, a slow Ca2+-driven phase 0 from about −40 mV and no distinct phases 1 and 2. Purkinje cells are fast response but show slow phase 4 depolarisation. Klabunde, Action potentials; SA node action potentials.
    3. What carries the plateau, and why does it matter?
    Inward L-type Ca2+ current balances outward K+ current. That calcium influx triggers calcium-induced calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum for contraction. The plateau prolongs the AP and the effective refractory period (phases 0 to 3 and early 4), which limits how quickly the cell can be re-excited. Klabunde, Non-pacemaker action potentials and Cardiac excitation-contraction coupling.
    4. How do sympathetic and vagal nerves change the heart rate?
    Noradrenaline on beta-adrenoceptors increases If and L-type Ca2+ current (beta-adrenergic stimulation also raises cAMP, which increases L-type Ca2+ entry): phase 4 steepens and threshold is reached sooner. Acetylcholine on muscarinic receptors increases K+ conductance (hyperpolarisation) and reduces If and Ca2+ current, so phase 4 starts lower and rises more slowly. At rest vagal tone dominates. Klabunde, Regulation of pacemaker activity and Cardiac excitation-contraction coupling.
    5. Explain the ECG of hyperkalaemia from the action potential.
    Raised extracellular K+ makes EK and the resting potential less negative (Nernst). The depolarised membrane inactivates fast Na+ channels, so phase 0 and conduction slow (wide QRS, longer PR, flatter P). In the SA node the pacemaker slope falls (bradycardia). T waves become tall and tented. Changes usually appear from about 6.0 mmol/L but the ECG correlates poorly with the potassium level. Klabunde, Membrane potentials, Non-pacemaker action potentials, and Regulation of pacemaker activity; LITFL, Hyperkalaemia.
    6. Why does hypokalaemia give U waves and an apparently long QT?
    Low extracellular K+ delays terminal ventricular repolarisation (the effects of serum potassium on individual currents are complex). T waves flatten or invert, U waves become prominent (best seen in V2 to V3) and fuse with the T wave, so the measured interval is really a long QU. LITFL, Hypokalaemia; Klabunde, Regulation of pacemaker activity, and Electrocardiogram (U wave as late ventricular repolarisation).
    7. How do classes Ia, Ib and Ic differ?
    All block fast Na+ channels and slow phase 0. Ic has the strongest effect and leaves APD and ERP little changed; Ia is intermediate and lengthens APD and ERP (K+ block); Ib has the weakest effect in normal tissue, shortens APD and ERP, and binds mainly inactivated channels, so it acts most in ischaemic tissue. Klabunde, Class I antiarrhythmics.
    8. On which phase do β-blockers, verapamil and adenosine act?
    On the nodal action potential. β-blockers flatten the SA phase 4 by removing sympathetic drive; verapamil and diltiazem flatten phase 4 and slow the Ca2+-driven phase 0; adenosine (A1, Gi) opens K+ channels, inhibits If and slows AV conduction. Ventricular phase 4 is a stable resting potential, not a pacemaker slope. Klabunde, CV Pharmacology: Beta-blockers, Calcium-channel blockers, Adenosine.
    9. Why do drugs that prolong the AP help re-entry but risk torsades?
    A longer ERP means a re-entering wave meets refractory tissue and dies out. A longer AP and QT can also allow afterdepolarisations, which trigger torsades de pointes, especially with long QT syndrome or hypokalaemia. Klabunde, Class III antiarrhythmics; BNF, Arrhythmias.
    10. Why does ischaemia slow conduction?
    Without ATP the Na+/K+-ATPase fails and K+ is lost, so the membrane depolarises. Depolarised cells have more inactivated Na+ channels (all are inactivated near −50 mV), so phase 0 is slower and smaller and conduction slows. Ischaemic cells may then fire slow Ca2+-dependent APs and show abnormal automaticity. Klabunde, Na+/K+-ATPase; Non-pacemaker action potentials.
